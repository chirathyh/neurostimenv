# H5 expert briefing and questions

## Purpose of this document

We are seeking advice from a computational neuroscientist on the most
scientifically defensible next experiment for Hypothesis 5 (H5): whether a
data-driven controller can use prestimulation EEG information to improve tACS
control beyond a strong, fixed signal-processing rule.

This is deliberately a **minimal mechanistic proof of concept**, not a model of
depression, a digital twin of a patient, or a clinical efficacy study. H1--H4
have established a sequence of results in an idealized 40-cell BallAndStick
network. Several exploratory H5 studies then tested whether observable
heterogeneity creates a useful controller-selection problem. Those studies
have not yet found a sufficiently large and reproducible conditional-treatment
opportunity to justify training a machine-learning policy.

The central question for expert review is therefore not “Which classifier
should we fit?” It is:

> What minimal, biologically plausible source of neural and/or measurement
> heterogeneity would create an EEG-observable, reproducible difference in the
> response to two or more safe tACS actions, without constructing the answer
> into the simulator?

We would particularly value advice on whether the current model is sufficient
for that question, which state and action variables are scientifically
meaningful, and where the simplest defensible boundary should be drawn.

---

## 1. Research question and hypotheses

### Overarching research question

**How can adaptive closed-loop neuromodulation treatment systems be designed
to help in neurological and psychiatric disorders?**

In this project, a BallAndStick network and weak-field tACS are used as a
transparent, computationally affordable example. The goal is to establish the
logic of observation, causal actuation, feedback, and learning in sequence.

### H1 -- an observable and targetable phenotype exists

Target disorders may exhibit observable, intervention-responsive neural
phenotypes for which a desired physiological target or direction of change can
be defined, providing a measurable objective for closed-loop
neuromodulation.

**Toy-model operationalization:** state A has an elevated, narrow-band alpha
component generated by weak rhythmic afferent-rate modulation; state B has
mean-rate-matched homogeneous afferent input. The task is to detect A from EEG
and define movement toward the population B EEG distribution. A and B are not
“depression” and “healthy.”

### H2 -- tACS causally modulates ongoing oscillations

A weak, spatially uniform tACS electric field can causally alter ongoing
network oscillatory dynamics and move an eligible A state toward the B
reference in an ideal neural-EEG endpoint.

### H3 -- one-step EEG conditioning improves stimulation selection

An EEG-conditioned, one-step rule that selects stimulation frequency and
EEG-relative phase can outperform state-independent stimulation strategies.

**Important scope:** the H3 method is deterministic signal processing, similar
in spirit to individualized-frequency and phase-tuned stimulation. It is not a
trained contextual bandit.

### H4 -- repeated causal feedback improves control of nonstationary phase

When the oscillatory phase is nonstationary, repeated causal EEG-based phase
updates can improve the modulation endpoint relative to one-time phase
initialization at the same frequency, montage, amplitude, and duration.

**Important scope:** H4 proves the value of deterministic adaptive phase
maintenance in this toy setting. It does not show that a learned policy is
needed.

### H5 -- data-driven learning improves over a strong fixed rule

Neural oscillations may contain biomarkers beyond carrier frequency and
instantaneous phase that predict differential response to available
stimulation strategies. A data-driven method should use those prestimulation
EEG biomarkers to select one safe action and improve the expected physiological
endpoint relative to the best fixed-rule method.

Our intended H5 comparison is stringent:

- the baseline should already use a robust EEG-only carrier-frequency
  estimator and causal phase tracking;
- the learned method must use only information available before its decision;
- it must improve over the **best fixed active strategy**, not merely sham or a
  deliberately poor random strategy;
- the improvement must be practically meaningful, reproducible across future
  stochastic realizations, and generalize to entirely unseen circuit
  structures; and
- the final claim must be tested on disjoint seeds after feature extraction,
  model class, hyperparameters, action set, endpoint, and decision rule have
  been frozen.

No H5 policy has yet been trained because the prerequisite response-mapping
studies did not establish a sufficiently strong learnable opportunity.

## 2. What the BallAndStick model contains

### 2.1 Cell population and geometry

The network contains 40 neurons:

- 32 excitatory (E) cells;
- 8 inhibitory (I) cells;
- each cell has one 30-µm soma and one 1000-µm apical cable;
- the somatodendritic axes are aligned with the positive z direction;
- somata occupy a compact population of approximately 100-µm radius, with a
  small z dispersion;
- the population is placed at approximately z = 78 mm inside the forward head
  model, and the scalp electrode is at z = 90 mm.

All E and I cells use the same canonical Hodgkin--Huxley somatic mechanism and
passive dendrite. E/I identity comes from the sign of the cell's outgoing
synapses and its population-specific afferent drive, not from distinct
interneuron and pyramidal-cell morphologies or channel repertoires. The
canonical HH model is run at its reference temperature of 6.3 °C; changing the
temperature to 36--37 °C without replacing/reparameterizing its kinetics would
not create a valid mammalian channel model.

The fixed simulation step is 0.0625 ms. This is much finer than needed for EEG
spectral resolution, but is used for stable, deterministic fixed-step NEURON
integration of the conductance-based cells and event-driven synapses.

### 2.2 Recurrent connectivity

The approximate connection probabilities are:

| Projection | Probability | Mean conductance | Sign |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| E to E | 0.10 | 0.001 µS | excitatory |
| E to I | 0.10 | 0.001 µS | excitatory |
| I to E | 0.50 | 0.010 µS | inhibitory |
| I to I | 0.50 | 0.010 µS | inhibitory |

Conductances have a coefficient of variation of approximately 0.1. Delays are
centered near 1.5 ms, with approximately 0.3-ms dispersion and a 0.3-ms lower
bound. Excitatory synapses use a fast conductance with a 0-mV reversal
potential; inhibitory synapses use a slower conductance with an approximately
-80-mV reversal potential.

Recurrent synapses are triggered by presynaptic spikes. **Poisson equations are
not applied to recurrent E/I synapses.** The manipulated stochastic processes
described below are used only for external/background afferent synapses.

### 2.3 Background/afferent input and the source of A/B rhythms

Each neuron receives 64 private excitatory afferent synapses. The mean
inter-event intervals are 40 ms for E cells and 30 ms for I cells, corresponding
to mean rates of approximately 25 and 33.3 events/s per synapse. Each synapse
has its own event stream and a mean conductance of 0.001 µS.

For a homogeneous Poisson input with rate \(\lambda\), the number of events in
an interval \(\Delta t\) follows

\[
N(t,t+\Delta t]\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta t),
\]

and the inter-event interval is exponentially distributed,

\[
T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda),\qquad
p(T)=\lambda e^{-\lambda T}.
\]

These are two equivalent descriptions of the same homogeneous Poisson point
process. For the rhythmic A state, the instantaneous afferent rate is

\[
\lambda_X(t)=\bar{\lambda}_X
\left[1+m\sin\phi(t)\right],
\qquad X\in\{E,I\},
\]

where \(m=0.04\) is a weak modulation depth and the expected time-averaged
rate remains \(\bar{\lambda}_X\). The same latent rate phase modulates the E
and I afferent populations, but individual synapses retain conditionally
independent Poisson event streams. Thus we impose weak population-level timing
structure without copying identical events across cells.

In the stationary H1--H3 model,

\[
\phi(t)=2\pi f t+\phi_0,
\qquad f\in\{9,11\}\ \mathrm{Hz},
\]

with seed-dependent initial phase \(\phi_0\). State B has \(m=0\), so its
afferents are homogeneous Poisson processes at exactly the same expected mean
rates.

In H4 and later nonstationary experiments, phase follows

\[
d\phi(t)=2\pi f\,dt+\sqrt{2D}\,dW_t,
\qquad D\in\{0.5,2.0\}\ \mathrm{rad^2/s}.
\]

The phase-increment variance over interval \(\tau\) is \(2D\tau\), while the
expected phase memory decays approximately as \(e^{-D\tau}\). This is a
standard minimal noisy-oscillator construction, not a claim that a specific
thalamic or cortical generator literally follows this SDE.

For the first H5 studies, we additionally varied the fraction
\(q\in\{0.5,1.0\}\) of afferents whose rates participate in the shared rhythmic
modulation. The remaining afferents use homogeneous, mean-rate-matched Poisson
input. This changes rhythmic-drive coherence without changing average afferent
rate.

### 2.4 What constitutes an independent replicate

We explicitly separate stochastic namespaces:

- a **structure seed** generates cell placement, connectivity, synaptic
  weights, and delays;
- an **afferent-history seed** generates the shared rate phase/path and private
  Poisson events before the decision;
- a **postdecision future seed** generates an independent continuation after
  the decision boundary; and
- in H5, an **observation-noise seed** generates the noisy EEG view.

The circuit structure is the statistical unit. Carrier, diffusion, shared
drive, observation-noise conditions, and future continuations are repeated
measurements within a structure. Adjacent EEG windows and multiple
postdecision futures are never treated as independent biological samples.

For causal action comparisons, all counterfactual arms share exactly the same
prestimulation network trajectory. Within a given future repeat they also
share the same stochastic continuation; only the stimulation action differs.
This common-random-number design removes avoidable Monte Carlo variance.

### 2.5 Simulated EEG

LFPy is still used for cell/network construction and for transforming membrane
currents into a current dipole. The population dipole is projected through an
LFPy four-sphere head model with approximate radii 79/80/85/90 mm and
conductivities 0.3/1.5/0.015/0.3 S/m. The output is in volts, band-pass filtered
from 0.5 to 100 Hz, and resampled to 500 Hz for analysis.

The resulting observation is an **ideal, single-channel, neural-only simulated
EEG**. H1--H4 do not contain sensor noise. H5 adds a controlled AR(1)
measurement-noise view for the estimator/controller while retaining ideal EEG
for the efficacy endpoint:

\[
\epsilon_t=\rho\epsilon_{t-1}+\eta_t,\qquad \rho=0.95,
\]

with RMS scaled to a fixed fraction of the prestimulation neural-EEG RMS.
Stimulation artifact, volume-conduction uncertainty, motion, eye/muscle
artifact, electrode drift, multichannel spatial filtering, and hardware
latency are not modeled. Therefore the work cannot yet make a simultaneous
tACS--EEG artifact-rejection or clinical sensing claim.

### 2.6 tACS actuator and causal online implementation

Scientific tACS experiments use a spatially uniform electric field, rather
than the legacy microscopic point-source approximation. In physical units,
the extracellular potential induced by a uniform field is

\[
V_e(\mathbf r,t)=-\mathbf E(t)\cdot
(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref}}).
\]

The primary montage is axial, aligned with the common somatodendritic z axis.
A transverse x-axis montage is used as an orientation control. The active
waveform has the form

\[
\mathbf E(t)=A\,w(t)\sin\theta(t)\,\hat{\mathbf z},
\]

where \(A\) is the tissue electric-field amplitude, \(w(t)\) is a ramp
envelope, and \(\theta(t)\) is the controller phase. Values used in the main
studies are 0.4 V/m for H2--H3 and 0.2 V/m for H4--H5; they do not represent a
scalp-current prescription. Isolated-cell tests verified signed,
orientation-dependent subthreshold polarization before network experiments.

Each online episode constructs the network and initializes it exactly once.
Burn-in, prestimulation EEG, intervention, and washout are consecutive windows
of one persistent NEURON trajectory. Membrane states, channel gates, synaptic
states, recurrence, and event queues are never reinitialized between control
decisions. The controller only uses EEG samples preceding each decision.

The refreshed controller uses the phase error \(e_k\) estimated from the
preceding EEG tail to apply a bounded carrier-frequency correction,

\[
\delta f_k=\operatorname{clip}\left(
\frac{e_k}{2\pi T_c},-2,+2\right)\ \mathrm{Hz},
\qquad T_c=0.25\ \mathrm{s}.
\]

The oscillator phase is integrated continuously, so an update changes the
instantaneous frequency over the next block instead of inserting an
unphysical phase jump. H4 froze a “fast” profile using 0.5 s of causal EEG and
updating every 125 ms. The comparison “slow” profile uses 1 s of EEG and
updates every 250 ms.

## 3. Established H1--H4 findings

### 3.1 H1--H3 stationary confirmatory design

The H1--H3 experiment used a stationary 9- or 11-Hz A generator and the
homogeneous B reference. A and B retained identical cells, recurrence,
synaptic weights, and expected afferent rates. The only A/B manipulation was
the event-time statistic of external afferents.

The protocol was:

1. 1 s burn-in;
2. 12 s stimulation-free prestimulation EEG;
3. 5 s intervention; and
4. 2 s washout.

The central 4 s of intervention was the outcome interval, avoiding ramp
transients. Paper PSDs used the full 4-s segment, a Hann taper, 50% overlap,
and 0.25-Hz frequency spacing. The primary spectral state was

\[
\mathbf x=(\log_{10}P_9,\log_{10}P_{11}),
\]

and movement was measured using standardized Euclidean distance to a frozen B
population mean. Improvement for an active arm was its paired sham distance
minus its active distance.

A separate set of 16 B seeds calibrated the population target. Sixteen new
candidate structures were characterized for H1. Eligibility for H2--H3 used
only prestimulation ideal EEG, not stimulation response, hidden generator
labels, or spikes. The prespecified screen required an elevated alpha state,
estimable phase, safe rates, and both 9- and 11-Hz contexts. All 16 passed; the
first 12, fixed prospectively, were enrolled.

For each enrolled structure and carrier, the H2 grid was:

| Action | Frequency | EEG-relative phase | Axial field |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sham | -- | -- | 0 V/m |
| 9-Hz in-phase | 9 Hz | 0 | 0.4 V/m |
| 9-Hz antiphase | 9 Hz | \(\pi\) | 0.4 V/m |
| 11-Hz in-phase | 11 Hz | 0 | 0.4 V/m |
| 11-Hz antiphase | 11 Hz | \(\pi\) | 0.4 V/m |

“Mismatched” was not a separate action. It denotes a 9-Hz action applied to an
11-Hz A context, or an 11-Hz action applied to a 9-Hz context. Four paired
postdecision futures estimated expected response. The main five-arm grid
therefore contained \(12\times2\times5\times4=480\) paired replays, plus
separate orientation and safety audits.

H3 froze an EEG-only rule from disjoint exploratory work: select the larger
9- versus 11-Hz prestimulation spectral excess and apply the matching carrier
at relative phase \(\pi\). It was compared with sham, the previously frozen
best fixed active action (9 Hz, \(\pi\)), and the uniform expected outcome of
the four active actions. It did not use a fitted ML model.

Structure was the inferential unit; carrier and futures were repeats. Primary
tests were prespecified one-sided exact sign-flip tests on structure-level
paired effects, with structure bootstrap intervals and parametric/nonparametric
sensitivity analyses. Frequency and phase interaction audits were
false-discovery-rate controlled.

### 3.2 H1--H3 results

| Hypothesis | Primary finding | Result |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Mean A-minus-B alpha power = 0.318 log10 units, bootstrap 95% CI [0.290, 0.343]; positive in 16/16 structures; exact \(p=1.53\times10^{-5}\). This is approximately a 2.08-fold power ratio. Held-reference balanced classification accuracy was 0.969, and EEG carrier identification was 100%. E and I rates remained matched. | **Confirmed** |
| H2 | Matched antiphase tACS improved target distance over sham by 1.724 standardized units, bootstrap 95% CI [1.636, 1.815]; positive in 12/12 structures; exact \(p=2.44\times10^{-4}\). Matching frequency improved over mismatching by 2.687 units, and antiphase improved over in-phase by 3.769 units; both FDR-adjusted \(q=2.44\times10^{-4}\). Axial stimulation exceeded the transverse orientation control. | **Confirmed** |
| H3 | The frozen EEG rule had mean expected distance 1.029 versus 2.349 for the best fixed active strategy, 3.357 for uniform random active stimulation, and 2.753 for sham. Its advantage over fixed was 1.320 [1.230, 1.409], and over random was 2.328 [2.258, 2.396]; both were positive in 12/12 structures with exact \(p=2.44\times10^{-4}\). A structure-preserving context shuffle failed. | **Confirmed** |

These results establish the intended logic in the stationary toy system, but
H3 is an easy frequency-identification problem: the relevant carrier is
explicitly visible in EEG, the response crossovers are large by construction,
and the deterministic frequency/phase rule is effectively oracle-optimal.
This is a useful individualized-frequency baseline, not evidence that machine
learning adds value.

H2's EEG effect should also not be overinterpreted as generic spike
desynchronization. The hidden E-population spike phase-coherence audit was not
directionally positive, while rate changes were small. The confirmed endpoint
is movement of the ideal forward-model EEG spectrum in this circuit.

### 3.3 H4 nonstationary confirmatory design

H4 retained the same weak mean-rate-matched A modulation, but replaced the
stationary phase with the phase-diffusion process above. It crossed 9/11 Hz
with low/high diffusion. The B target remained a separately calibrated
homogeneous-input population reference.

The final protocol was:

1. 1 s burn-in;
2. 12 s stimulation-free context EEG;
3. 9 s intervention, with a central duration-matched 8-s primary EEG endpoint;
4. 2 s washout.

All active arms used the EEG-selected carrier, an axial 0.2-V/m field, a
\(\pi\)-relative target, the same 1-s phase initialization, 0.5-s ramps, and
the same bounded-frequency continuous-waveform controller. The three arms
were sham, one-time phase initialization, and the frozen 0.5-s-history/125-ms
refresh controller.

Controller development and confirmation were strictly separated. D0/D0b
validated observability of phase diffusion; D1 and D1-R exposed instability;
two bandwidth studies then froze the 0.5-s/125-ms controller without using the
final confirmation seeds. H4 confirmation screened 16 new structures, enrolled
12, crossed each with two carriers and two diffusion levels, and used six
paired future continuations:

\[
12\ \text{structures}\times2f\times2D\times3\ \text{arms}
\times6\ \text{futures}=864\ \text{replays}.
\]

The primary endpoint was the structure-level mean advantage of refreshed over
one-time control in absolute log10 alpha-power distance to B. The a priori
design used one-sided \(\alpha=0.05\), 80% power for a standardized paired
effect \(d_z=0.8\), and 12 independent structures. The exact sign-flip test was
primary; t intervals, structure bootstrap intervals, and Wilcoxon tests were
sensitivity analyses. Refreshed-versus-sham was tested only after the primary
contrast passed.

### 3.4 H4 results

Mean distance to B was 0.3207 for one-time control, 0.2855 for sham, and 0.2188
for refreshed control. The refreshed-minus-one-time advantage was 0.10191
log10-distance units (SD 0.01772, SE 0.00511, \(d_z=5.75\)), positive in all
12 structures. The 95% t interval was [0.09065, 0.11316], the structure
bootstrap interval was [0.09331, 0.11213], and the exact one-sided sign-flip
test gave \(p=0.000244\). Refreshed control also improved over sham by 0.0667
units in all 12 structures. One-time phase initialization was worse than sham
on average by 0.0352 units.

The common causal phase auditor found mean absolute phase error of 1.845 rad
for one-time control and 0.680 rad for refreshed control. The error reduction
was 1.164 rad and was positive in every structure. Refreshed control beat
one-time in 256/288 paired future comparisons (88.9%) and sham in 245/288
(85.1%). Benefit remained positive at both diffusion levels, though it was
larger at low diffusion.

The selected controller made approximately 71 post-onset corrections during
each 9-s intervention. Mean absolute frequency correction was 0.588 Hz,
frequency bounds were respected, waveform discontinuity was at numerical
precision, and all arms were rate-safe. Final extracellular field was zero and
washout recovery passed. The intervention improved but did not make A
equivalent to B.

**H4 adaptive phase maintenance was confirmed.** The inference is limited to
deterministic feedback in artifact-free simulated EEG under an engineered
phase-diffusion generator. It does not establish that this specific controller
is clinically optimal or that adaptation is superior to every conceivable
open-loop waveform.

## 4. What has been attempted for H5

### 4.1 Intended H5 decision problem

H4 supplied a strong responsive controller profile:

- **fast profile:** 0.5-s causal phase history, updates every 125 ms;
- **slow profile:** 1.0-s causal phase history, updates every 250 ms.

Both use exactly the same carrier estimator, EEG-relative antiphase target,
axial montage, 0.2-V/m amplitude, initialization, correction horizon, and
frequency-slew bound. For any intervention, one profile is selected once and
used for the entire intervention; profiles are not mixed.

The hoped-for H5 mechanism was a bias--variance trade-off:

- rapid/high-diffusion phase should favor the fast tracker because older
  samples become stale;
- noisier observations and slower dynamics might favor the slow tracker
  because its longer history averages measurement noise; and
- prestimulation EEG features such as linewidth, phase-increment coherence,
  phase-estimate stability, and spectral SNR might reveal which regime is
  present.

If the two profiles had reproducible response crossovers, one could train an
interpretable model to predict the better single profile from prestimulation
EEG. We deliberately required this full-information oracle opportunity before
fitting a policy: a classifier cannot create treatment benefit when one action
is essentially dominant or when labels are stochastic.

### 4.2 H5-P0: shared-drive controller-profile feasibility

P0 crossed:

- carrier \(f\in\{9,11\}\) Hz;
- diffusion \(D\in\{0.5,2.0\}\) rad²/s; and
- shared rhythmic-afferent fraction \(q\in\{0.5,1.0\}\).

Mean afferent rates were matched and private Poisson event streams remained
independent. A frozen AR(1) observation had \(\rho=0.95\) and RMS equal to 25%
of prestimulation neural-EEG RMS. Noisy EEG was supplied to the carrier
estimator, context features, and phase tracker; efficacy was assessed on ideal
neural EEG. Sham, slow, and fast profiles were evaluated with paired futures.

The shared-drive level was observable from phase-invariant EEG using a
leave-one-structure-out audit (balanced accuracy 0.859). Both profiles were
occasionally oracle-optimal, but the best fixed profile was fast (expected
distance 0.18617), while the full-information oracle reached only 0.17975.
The mean oracle gain was 0.00680 log10 units, below the prespecified 0.01
practical threshold. The realized optimal profile agreed across futures only
64.3% of the time. Carrier identification also failed its gate. P0 therefore
stopped before policy training.

**Interpretation:** \(q\) changed observable EEG and relative controller
response, but not enough to make choosing a profile reliably valuable.

### 4.3 H5-I0 and H5-I0b: robust carrier measurement

Because erroneous 9/11-Hz selection would confound response mapping, we
separated measurement validation from stimulation.

H5-I0 retained 30 s of stimulation-free noisy EEG and compared prespecified
smoothed-log-Welch and Gaussian-peak estimators. Neither met the discovery
coverage/accuracy gate; no confirmation data were used and no estimator was
selected.

H5-I0b preserved that negative result, used new structures, and compared a
pooled multitaper-evidence method designed for diffuse peaks. The selected
estimator used DPSS multitapers, aperiodic-corrected local evidence around only
the two available actions (9 and 11 Hz), robust aggregation across 6-s
overlapping windows, and an explicit abstention/fallback rule. It was selected
on six discovery structures and frozen before 12 disjoint confirmation
structures. All runs were stimulation-free.

Confirmation observed-EEG carrier accuracy was 0.9271 with bootstrap 95% CI
[0.8854, 0.9688], while preserving performance across frequency, diffusion,
shared-drive, and structure strata. Recent phase remained actionable.

**Interpretation:** robust toy carrier measurement was validated, but this is
not clinical IAF validation and does not establish a response-conditioned H5
opportunity.

### 4.4 H5-P1: active response mapping after fixing measurement

P1 used six new structures, the full 9/11-Hz by low/high-diffusion by
partial/full-shared-drive grid, 30 s of stimulation-free noisy EEG, 8 s of
intervention, 1 s washout, and four paired futures per action. Abstention from
the frozen multitaper estimator mapped to sham. Slow and fast active profiles
used the same 0.2-V/m field and ideal neural EEG remained the efficacy
endpoint.

Accepted carrier accuracy was high (0.9474), but enrollment/coverage was only
0.75. The fast profile again had the best fixed expected distance (0.18237),
slow had 0.19260, and the post-hoc oracle had 0.17863. Oracle advantage over
fast was only 0.00395, below 0.01. The expected winner was fast in 28 contexts
and slow in 8, but realized future agreement was approximately 0.70. No
prestimulation EEG feature survived FDR correction for association with
relative response.

**Interpretation:** repairing carrier measurement did not repair the causal
response-map problem. Fast control remained close to dominant, and residual
action differences were small/noisy.

### 4.5 H5-P2A: stimulation-free phase-tracker bias--variance audit

P2A isolated measurement mechanics from tACS response. It fixed \(q=1\), used
six new structures crossed with 9/11 Hz and both diffusion levels (24 neural
network episodes), and gave every neural episode paired AR(1) noise views at
RMS fractions 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75. Each episode contained 30 s for carrier
measurement and a later 8-s stimulation-free tracker evaluation. The same unit
noise path was scaled across noise conditions. The hidden afferent phase was
used only as a mechanistic audit, never as tracker input.

The 0.50 noise condition was selected as the “high-noise” level. At low noise
and high diffusion, fast tracking reduced phase error by 0.172 rad and was
better in 6/6 structures. At high noise and low diffusion, slow tracking had a
0.029-rad advantage and was better in 4/6 structures. The resulting measurement
crossover was 0.200 rad. The 0.75 condition was rejected because carrier
measurement deteriorated.

**Interpretation:** the desired signal-processing bias--variance trade-off can
exist at the phase-estimation level. P2A contained no stimulation, however, so
it could not establish that the phase-error crossover affects the neural EEG
endpoint.

### 4.6 H5-P2B: active test of the phase-tracker trade-off

P2B froze the P2A conditions and used six entirely new structures crossed with
9/11 Hz, low/high diffusion, and 0.25/0.50 observation-noise fractions. It
screened 48 contexts; 43 were eligible. Each eligible context received sham,
slow, and fast profiles over four paired futures, giving 516 action-future
outcomes. The protocol was 1 s burn-in, 30 s prestimulation noisy EEG, 8 s
intervention, and 1 s washout.

Mean target distances were:

| Arm | Mean distance to B | Mean improvement over sham |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sham | 0.26517 | -- |
| Slow 1-s/250-ms profile | 0.24801 | 0.01717 |
| Fast 0.5-s/125-ms profile | 0.23556 | 0.02962 |

Fast-minus-slow efficacy advantages, expressed as slow distance minus fast
distance, were positive in every prespecified corner:

| Diffusion | Observation noise | Fast advantage |
|---|---:|---:|
| High | 0.25 | 0.02280 |
| High | 0.50 | 0.01189 |
| Low | 0.25 | 0.01102 |
| Low | 0.50 | 0.00561 |

Thus the required slow-profile advantage at low diffusion/high noise did not
occur. The response crossover had the wrong sign and a magnitude of 0.0172,
below the prespecified 0.02 gate. Averaged post hoc at the structure level,
fast exceeded slow by about 0.0126 units in all six structures, but that is
evidence for a strong fixed fast controller, not context-dependent selection.

The best fixed active strategy (fast) had mean distance 0.23556; an active-only
post-hoc oracle reached 0.23370, a gain of only 0.00184. Although the oracle
selected fast in 30 contexts and slow in 13, the tiny gain shows that most
selection changes were not practically consequential. Realized optimal-profile
agreement across futures was 0.645, below the 0.75 reliability gate. Phase-error
advantage did not predict tACS-response advantage (Spearman \(\rho\approx
0.009\)). None of 21 prestimulation EEG feature-response associations survived
FDR correction.

Carrier measurement remained good but imperfect. At noise 0.25, coverage was
0.958 and accepted accuracy was 1.0. At noise 0.50, coverage was 0.917 and
accepted accuracy was 0.864. Three accepted high-diffusion/high-noise contexts
were incorrectly mapped from 9 to 11 Hz; stimulation in those cases was
materially harmful relative to sham. This emphasizes the practical importance
of calibrated abstention and safe fallback.

All firing rates remained within the predefined safe range, frequency
corrections stayed within ±2 Hz, waveform discontinuities were at numerical
precision, and final extracellular field returned to zero.

Two implementation audits also failed and must be repaired before any future
confirmation:

1. the “identical standardized noise path” hash failed in 15/24 paired
   contexts. Inspection indicates that the same deterministic unit-noise seed
   and neural baseline were used, but the audit reconstructed standardized
   noise from floating-point subtraction after scale changes. The next version
   should save and hash the original unit-noise vector directly;
2. one of 516 physiological washout-recovery comparisons exceeded a strict
   tolerance by a very small amount, although the final applied extracellular
   field was exactly zero. Future code should report physical field removal
   separately from statistical recovery of the neural endpoint.

Neither audit issue explains away the primary negative result. Even if both
are treated as diagnostic implementation issues, P2B found no practically
meaningful, observable, reliable profile-selection opportunity.

**H5-P2B did not pass. No H5-P2C policy was trained.**

### 4.7 Current H5 conclusion

The negative H5 studies have separated three concepts that are easily
conflated:

1. **Observability:** shared-drive level, phase diffusion, carrier, and some
   phase-tracker properties can be inferred from prestimulation noisy EEG.
2. **Controller mechanics:** fast and slow phase estimators can exhibit a
   measurable bias--variance crossover without stimulation.
3. **Conditional controllability:** those observable/mechanical differences do
   not necessarily produce a large, stable crossover in the neural response to
   tACS.

The present bottleneck is item 3. The fast H4 controller is a strong fixed
baseline and is nearly dominant at 0.2 V/m. A more complex model cannot recover
meaningful benefit from an action space in which expected action values are
nearly equal or one action dominates. Training ML on the current data would
primarily learn Monte Carlo future noise and would overstate H5.

## 5. Scientific constraints for the next H5 design

We would like the next study to respect the following boundaries.

1. **Keep the system minimal.** It must remain practical to simulate on a CPU
   workstation. A small number of discrete, interpretable actions is preferred.
2. **Do not call A/B depression/healthy.** The model may illustrate a
   disorder-relevant oscillatory control concept, but it is not a validated
   disorder microcircuit.
3. **Do not manufacture an ML win.** We do not want to encode a direct rule such
   as “latent state 1 responds to action 1” unless that interaction emerges
   from a defensible biophysical mechanism.
4. **Use a strong comparator.** The fixed-rule baseline should include the
   confirmed multitaper carrier estimate, causal EEG phase tracking, and the
   best fixed controller/action. Sham is a causal reference, not the only
   comparator.
5. **Separate state generation from actuation.** tACS must not directly modify
   the code variable used to create the afferent rhythm.
6. **Use deployable observations.** The policy may use only causal, noisy EEG
   and action history. Hidden frequency, diffusion, shared-drive fraction,
   latent phase, spikes, and rates can be used only for post-hoc mechanistic or
   safety audits.
7. **Preserve prospective screening.** Eligibility may identify whether the
   target phenotype and carrier are measurable, but cannot use action outcomes
   or a seed-specific B counterfactual.
8. **Require opportunity before learning.** On discovery seeds, a
   full-information expected-outcome oracle must show both action use, practical
   improvement over the best fixed strategy, cross-structure replication, and
   future-wise label reliability. Otherwise stop.
9. **Use disjoint development and confirmation.** Generator/action design,
   feature pipeline, learner, tuning, and endpoint are frozen before final
   structure seeds.
10. **Treat structure as the inferential unit.** Histories, frequencies,
    regimes, windows, and paired futures are repeats. They may improve response
    estimates but not inflate sample size.
11. **Retain causal pairing and safety.** Counterfactual actions should share
    predecision trajectories and future randomness. Include sham, rate safety,
    field-zero, washout, frequency-bound, and waveform-continuity audits.
12. **Keep claims proportional.** Ideal neural EEG may establish an in-silico
    mechanism. Clinical relevance would still require realistic morphology,
    orientation, tissue fields, artifacts, latency, multichannel sensing, and
    empirical validation.

The current project is willing to add a small number of biologically motivated
heterogeneity axes, additional causal EEG features, realistic observation
noise/latency, or a slightly richer discrete action set. It is not intended to
become a full L23Net-scale disease microcircuit or a large clinical simulator.

## 6. Candidate directions for expert evaluation

These are possibilities, not decisions. We want expert advice on which, if any,
has the strongest biological and control-theoretic justification.

### Direction A -- time-varying dynamical regimes and controller parameters

Allow diffusion, rhythmic coherence, or oscillatory amplitude to change slowly
within an episode, producing bursts or transitions rather than a single frozen
regime. The observable context could include causal phase-innovation variance,
linewidth, spectral entropy, amplitude envelope, and phase-estimate
confidence. An action could select a tracker gain/history/cadence for the next
block.

This is closer to a genuinely sequential controller than selecting one profile
for an entire intervention. However, it risks building an artificial switching
task, and P2B warns that estimator-error differences need not produce neural
response differences.

### Direction B -- stimulation amplitude or phase-offset conditioned on
oscillator susceptibility

Retain robust individualized frequency and continuous phase tracking, but let a
policy select one of a small number of field amplitudes (including sham) or
relative phase offsets. Candidate EEG moderators include peak prominence,
linewidth, coherent-versus-broadband alpha energy, phase-increment coherence,
and baseline distance to B. From weakly forced-oscillator theory, response can
depend on detuning, endogenous coherence, phase response, and forcing strength,
so a dose or offset interaction is plausible.

Earlier amplitude experiments did not generalize, so this direction should
begin with a small disjoint full-information response map. We would need a
clear rationale for why the simple cells should exhibit the desired crossover,
not merely a wider grid search.

### Direction C -- biologically plausible response heterogeneity at fixed
baseline phenotype

Construct multiple A circuits with similar observable alpha excess but modest
differences in recurrent E/I balance, intrinsic excitability, synaptic time
constants, afferent coherence, or somatodendritic orientation. These factors
could alter both spectral shape and field susceptibility. The learning task
would be to infer a response-relevant phenotype from EEG, not to identify an
explicit generator label.

This may produce the clearest biological reason for individualized response,
but it has the greatest risk of overcomplicating the toy model or confounding
the target phenotype. Some variables, especially orientation or tissue-field
coupling, may not be identifiable from a single EEG channel and would require
anatomical covariates rather than EEG.

### Direction D -- treat versus abstain under calibrated uncertainty

P2B suggests that carrier errors at high observation noise can make active
stimulation harmful. A policy could choose fast active control or safe sham
based on carrier confidence and other EEG-quality measures. A post-hoc oracle
including sham had a larger, though still subthreshold, opportunity than the
active-only oracle.

This is practically important and resembles selective prediction/safe
decision-making. It may, however, demonstrate measurement-quality triage rather
than the intended claim that neural biomarkers predict differential biological
response. We need advice on whether this is an acceptable H5 interpretation or
should remain a safety subsystem alongside a separate efficacy policy.

### Direction E -- stronger model-based baseline and residual learning

A state-space oscillator, Kalman filter, phase-locked loop, or Bayesian tracker
could jointly estimate phase, frequency drift, coherence, and uncertainty. A
learned method would then need to improve stimulation decisions beyond that
model-based controller, perhaps by learning residual response heterogeneity.

This makes the comparator scientifically strong, but it may make “machine
learning improves over fixed rule” harder to establish and may change H5 into a
system-identification comparison rather than a contextual-bandit question.

## 7. Questions for the computational-neuroscience expert

### A. Is the scientific framing sound?

1. Is H5, as written, a defensible hypothesis for this toy model, or should it
   be reframed more narrowly as **EEG-conditioned treatment-effect
   heterogeneity** rather than “machine learning improves neuromodulation”?
2. Is it appropriate to require improvement over a robust individualized
   carrier plus continuous phase-tracking baseline, rather than only sham,
   random stimulation, or a fixed population frequency?
3. Does the A-versus-B construction provide a scientifically acceptable
   oscillatory control target, provided it is explicitly described as a
   mean-rate-matched afferent-timing toy phenotype and not depression?
4. Is distance to the homogeneous-input B alpha-power distribution a reasonable
   endpoint, or should the objective be directional suppression with a bounded
   target range to prevent overshoot?

### B. What neural heterogeneity is minimally biological and useful?

5. Is shared phase diffusion a reasonable minimal representation of fluctuating
   population-level afferent timing? What biological process should we cite as
   the abstraction: distributed afferent phase noise, finite-coherence
   oscillatory drive, or something else?
6. Is varying the fraction of rhythmically modulated afferents \(q\) a
   defensible representation of coherent-versus-asynchronous afferent
   recruitment? P0/P1 showed that it is EEG-observable but not a strong
   controller-response moderator. Should this variable now be dropped?
7. Which one minimal extension is most likely to create a genuine and
   interpretable state-by-action interaction:
   - slow time variation in \(D\), frequency, or modulation depth;
   - amplitude-envelope/burst dynamics;
   - two competing nearby oscillatory sources;
   - E- and I-afferent phase/coherence differences;
   - modest recurrent E/I or synaptic-time-constant heterogeneity;
   - modest intrinsic excitability or membrane-time-constant heterogeneity; or
   - heterogeneous cell orientation/field coupling?
8. Can any of those changes be justified in a network where all cells share the
   same canonical HH soma and simple apical cable, or would such an extension
   demand a more detailed cell/network model before the result is credible?
9. How can we vary response susceptibility while keeping the baseline A
   phenotype and firing-rate range comparable, so the learner is not merely
   classifying obvious amplitude or rate differences?
10. Would an afferent-driven rhythm be too directly controllable/unrealistic for
    H5? Should the target rhythm instead arise partly from recurrent E/I
    dynamics, even if that increases model complexity and runtime?

### C. What should the action space be?

11. Does selecting between a 0.5-s/125-ms and 1-s/250-ms phase controller have
    a plausible biological/engineering interpretation, or is it primarily a
    signal-processing choice with too little influence on neuronal response?
12. Given P2B's near-dominance of the fast controller, should we abandon this
    action pair rather than amplify sensor noise until a crossover appears?
13. Which small action set is most defensible for H5:
    - fast controller versus sham/abstain;
    - two safe field amplitudes plus sham;
    - two EEG-relative phase offsets plus sham;
    - two controller gains/correction horizons;
    - a frequency neighborhood around the estimated carrier; or
    - two composite but prespecified controller profiles?
14. At 0.2 V/m the fast profile is effective and nearly dominant. Is there a
    first-principles reason to expect amplitude, phase offset, or control gain
    to interact with coherence/diffusion in this type of weakly forced network?
15. Should the selected action remain fixed for the full 8-s intervention, or
    would a blockwise action every 0.5--1 s be more biologically and practically
    meaningful? If blockwise, how should we distinguish a contextual bandit
    from a dynamical state-feedback or reinforcement-learning problem?

### D. What EEG context is meaningful and causally available?

16. Are the following phase-invariant causal features sensible: carrier
    evidence/confidence, peak prominence, linewidth, spectral entropy,
    coherent versus residual alpha energy, alpha/off-carrier power ratio,
    phase-increment coherence, phase-innovation variance, phase-actionability
    fraction, amplitude-envelope variance, and recent nonstationarity?
17. Which of these features best distinguishes **process noise** in the neural
    oscillator from **measurement noise** in the sensor? Is that distinction
    realistically identifiable from a single 30-s channel?
18. Would a state-space oscillator/PLL/Kalman model provide a more principled
    context than hand-crafted Welch/multitaper summaries? If so, which latent
    state and uncertainty outputs should be exposed to the decision rule?
19. Is a 30-s prestimulation record reasonable for 9/11-Hz separation and
    slow-dynamics estimation in a toy experiment? What duration would balance
    stationarity, resolution, and practical closed-loop use?
20. Should we add realistic causal latency, packet loss, electrode drift, or
    nonstationary observation noise now, or would that obscure the neural H5
    question before an ideal-EEG opportunity is established?
21. Is a single EEG channel sufficient for the proposed proof of concept? If
    not, what minimal multichannel or source-projected extension would add
    genuinely relevant information without making this a head-model study?

### E. What learner and estimand would be appropriate?

22. If one action is chosen per episode, is the correct formulation a
    contextual bandit, supervised individualized-treatment-rule estimation, or
    conditional average treatment-effect estimation? What distinction matters
    most for the paper?
23. Should the initial learner be an interpretable regularized linear/logistic
    model, shallow decision tree, Bayesian model, or a small nonlinear model?
    We prefer the lowest-capacity method that can represent the prespecified
    interaction.
24. Should training labels be the better action based on paired mean outcomes,
    or should the model directly regress each action's expected distance to B?
    How should uncertainty in the four-to-six future repeats enter training?
25. Would a doubly robust/off-policy contextual-bandit evaluation add anything
    when the simulator can provide randomized, paired, full-information
    counterfactuals during development?
26. What effect size should count as practically meaningful in log10 alpha
    distance? Our current 0.01 threshold was prespecified but not biologically
    calibrated. Should it be related to within-trajectory EEG variability,
    A--B separation, or a fraction of H4's 0.102-unit benefit?
27. What minimum action-label reliability across stochastic futures is
    reasonable before policy fitting? We used 0.75 and failed at approximately
    0.65 in P2B.

### F. How should discovery and confirmation be powered?

28. How many independent circuit structures are needed for a credible H5
    discovery/confirmation sequence? H1 used 16 structures; H2--H4 used 12 for
    confirmation; H5 exploration has so far used six structures with crossed
    repeated conditions.
29. Would you recommend two nested stages:
    (i) disjoint full-information opportunity mapping, then
    (ii) frozen-policy confirmation on 12--20 new structures? Should model
    selection use leave-one-structure-out cross-validation?
30. What should be the single primary contrast: learned policy versus best
    fixed active controller, versus the robust individualized-frequency
    clinical-style rule, or both in a fixed testing sequence?
31. Which secondary comparisons are essential: sham, random actions,
    context-shuffled policy, oracle upper bound, and/or a model-based adaptive
    controller?
32. Should generalization be required separately across carrier, diffusion,
    response-heterogeneity level, observation quality, and circuit structure,
    or would this create an impractically large factorial design?

### G. What would count as a convincing negative or positive result?

33. If no biologically defensible action crossover exists in this simple
    network, is the correct conclusion that H5 cannot be demonstrated without a
    richer circuit, rather than continuing to add engineered stochasticity?
34. What evidence would convince you that a learned-policy advantage reflects a
    neural response biomarker rather than carrier-estimation confidence,
    regression to the mean, screening leakage, or stochastic future noise?
35. Would a safe **treat-versus-abstain** policy based on EEG confidence be a
    meaningful H5 result, or should it be described separately as measurement
    quality control?
36. Which mechanistic audits would you require to support an H5 interpretation:
    membrane polarization, phase error, coherent alpha suppression, spike
    phase locking, unchanged mean firing rate, dose response, orientation
    dependence, or something else?

## 8. Proposed decision sequence after expert feedback

Unless the expert recommends a different route, our preferred conservative
workflow is:

1. choose one minimal, biologically justified response-heterogeneity mechanism
   and one small action set;
2. fix the P2B noise-hash and field-removal diagnostics;
3. run a low-cost, disjoint, full-information response map with paired futures;
4. require a practical oracle gap, both-action support, cross-structure
   consistency, future-wise reliability, and an observable EEG-response
   association;
5. stop if that gate fails;
6. if it passes, predeclare a low-capacity EEG-only learner and nested
   structure-level validation procedure;
7. freeze the complete policy and compare it on new structures against the
   best fixed active rule, sham, and relevant signal-processing baseline; and
8. report any H5 result explicitly as a toy in-silico proof of conditional
   control, not a clinical-treatment claim.
